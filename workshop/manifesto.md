# Instrument Design Manifesto

## Purpose

This manifesto defines the principles that guide the design and creation of our instruments.

It is not a build manual and it is not a collection of fixed rules. It describes how we think.

The aim is to create instruments that are thoughtful, engaging, beautiful, highly playable and genuinely rewarding to own. Just as importantly, the process of creating them should be enjoyable, curious and collaborative.

This is a living manifesto. It will evolve as experience teaches us better ways to design, build and think.

## 1. Start with Why

Every significant design choice should have a reason.

Tradition is valuable, but "that is how it is normally done" is not, by itself, sufficient justification.

We seek to understand the underlying principles before choosing an implementation.

If a decision cannot yet be explained, it remains open.

## 2. One Decision at a Time

Important design decisions deserve focused attention.

For each decision we will:

- define the goal;
- explore the realistic design space;
- understand the advantages and disadvantages of each option;
- consider risks and consequences;
- make a deliberate choice;
- record why that choice was made.

We do not rush decisions simply to make progress.

## 3. Every Decision Is a Trade-Off

There is rarely a single perfect solution.

Strength may compete with weight. Simplicity may compete with adjustability. Tradition may compete with innovation. Tone may compete with durability.

Good design is not the elimination of compromise. It is the conscious selection of the compromises that best serve the instrument.

## 4. Understand the Primary Purpose

Every component has a primary job.

Before choosing its material, shape, construction or appearance, we first ask:

**What is this component trying to achieve?**

Secondary functions matter, but they should not obscure or compromise the primary purpose.

## 5. Principles Before Implementations

Different instruments may solve the same underlying problem in very different ways.

A cigar box guitar neck and a steel-string acoustic neck may share fundamental structural principles while requiring very different implementations.

We therefore distinguish between:

- enduring principles; and
- instrument-specific design choices.

This allows knowledge to be reused without assuming that one solution fits every instrument.

## 6. Build Instruments People Want to Pick Up

Technical correctness is not enough.

An instrument should invite interaction.

It should feel comfortable, look intentional, respond beautifully and reward the player when they engage with it.

The goal is not merely to build an instrument that works.

The goal is to build one that people do not want to put down.

## 7. Handmade, Not Merely Homemade

Hand construction is not an excuse for poor precision or unfinished thinking.

We value the individuality of handmade work while aspiring to professional standards of:

- playability;
- reliability;
- fit and finish;
- maintainability;
- durability.

The instrument should feel considered rather than improvised.

## 8. The Journey Matters

Design and construction should be enjoyable.

Curiosity is encouraged. Detours are welcome when they deepen understanding.

The finished instrument matters, but so does everything learned while creating it.

For commissioned instruments, the client should be able to participate meaningfully in that journey. The design process itself should be engaging and rewarding.

## 9. Build for Learning

Every instrument is both a finished object and an experiment.

We record what worked, what did not, what surprised us and what we would change next time.

The first instrument establishes a reference point.

Future instruments evolve from evidence and experience rather than starting again from zero.

## 10. Let Processes Earn Their Place

Processes, templates and standards are not adopted simply because they sound useful.

They are tried in practice.

If they help, they are refined and retained.

If they create friction without sufficient value, they are changed or removed.

Documentation exists to preserve useful knowledge, not to satisfy a process.

## 11. Refactor Freely

The workshop itself is a design.

Its knowledge structure, templates, standards and processes are expected to evolve.

When experience reveals a better way to organise or work, we change it.

Nothing is sacred simply because it was decided first.

## 12. Strong Decisions, Open Minds

Once a decision is made, we commit to it.

But decisions are based on the best understanding available at the time, not on pride or permanence.

Better evidence can justify a better decision.

The goal is not to prove that an earlier choice was right.

The goal is to keep improving.

## The Standard We Aspire To

An instrument should never leave someone thinking:

> "That is impressive for something handmade."

The aspiration is simpler:

> **"That is an amazing instrument."**

Everything else follows from there.

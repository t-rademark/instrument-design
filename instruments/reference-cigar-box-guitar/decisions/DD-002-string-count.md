# DD-002: String Count

**Status:** Approved  
**Date:** 2026-08-07

## The Question

How many strings should the reference cigar box guitar have?

String count affects not only the musical range of the instrument, but also its character, playability, neck dimensions, string tension, hardware requirements and construction complexity.

The choice should therefore be made according to the mission of the reference instrument rather than convention alone.

## Goals

The chosen string count should:

- retain a clear cigar box guitar character rather than simply reproducing a conventional guitar in a smaller form;
- provide enough musical range to be genuinely rewarding to play;
- suit a blues-focused instrument intended for riffs, rhythm, slide and groove;
- support both slide and fretted playing;
- expose useful lessons about tuning, intervals and harmony;
- keep the first build understandable enough that the contribution of each string can be clearly explored;
- avoid unnecessary construction complexity;
- provide enough capability that the finished instrument remains desirable rather than feeling like a training exercise;
- allow a relatively inexperienced player to develop simple but musically compelling material that showcases the instrument;
- provide useful experience that can inform different string counts in future instruments.

## Design Space

### Option 1: Three Strings

Use the traditional minimalist three-string cigar box guitar format.

Three strings provide enough musical information to support useful chord tunings, riffs, rhythm and melodic playing while keeping the instrument deliberately simple.

Depending on the chosen tuning, the strings could form a complete chord or deliberately omit particular chord tones to create a more open or ambiguous sound.

**Advantages**

- strong cigar box guitar identity;
- well suited to riffs, rhythm, slide and groove;
- relatively low total string tension;
- allows a narrower and potentially comfortable neck;
- mechanically and visually simple;
- makes the musical role of each string particularly clear;
- encourages deliberate exploration of tuning and intervals;
- offers enough capability for simple but compelling showcase playing;
- reinforces the distinctive character of the reference instrument.

**Disadvantages**

- reduced musical range compared with four or six strings;
- fewer conventional chord voicings;
- less direct transfer to standard six-string guitar technique;
- some harmonic possibilities require compromises or alternative tunings.

### Option 2: Four Strings

Add a fourth string to increase musical range and tuning possibilities while retaining much of the simplicity and character of a cigar box guitar.

**Advantages**

- broader musical range than three strings;
- additional chord and melodic possibilities;
- greater tuning flexibility;
- still compact and recognisably a cigar box guitar;
- well suited to blues and slide;
- potentially more versatile as the player's skills develop.

**Disadvantages**

- greater total string tension;
- may require a wider neck or reduced string spacing;
- additional complexity in the nut, bridge, tuners and pickup;
- slightly reduces the clarity of each individual string's musical role;
- adds capability that may not be required for the intended use;
- moves away from the deliberate minimalism of the three-string format.

### Option 3: Six Strings

Use the conventional six-string guitar format.

This would provide the broadest musical range and the strongest compatibility with standard guitar techniques and repertoire.

**Advantages**

- greatest musical range;
- widest choice of chord voicings and melodic possibilities;
- familiar conventional guitar technique;
- skills transfer directly between the reference instrument and a standard guitar;
- significant room for musical development as the player improves.

**Disadvantages**

- substantially greater string tension and structural demand;
- wider neck and more hardware;
- greater complexity in the bridge, nut, tuners and pickup;
- requires more technique to make effective use of the additional strings;
- risks making the instrument feel like a homemade conventional guitar rather than a distinctive cigar box guitar;
- adds significant capability that is not required by the reference instrument's mission.

## Trade-Offs

The central trade-off is between **musical range and versatility** on one side and **simplicity, identity and immediacy** on the other.

More strings provide access to more notes, chord voicings and conventional guitar techniques.

However, musical expressiveness does not depend solely on the number of strings available.

For the intended blues-focused use of this instrument, rhythm, phrasing, tone, dynamics, slide technique and groove are expected to contribute strongly to its musical character.

Three strings reduce the available range, but that limitation is acceptable for the reference instrument's intended use.

The constraint may also provide useful learning value by making the contribution of each string, interval and tuning choice easier to hear and understand.

Four strings provide a credible middle ground, but the additional range does not currently justify moving away from the simplicity of three strings.

Six strings provide capability beyond the requirements of the reference instrument and risk weakening its cigar box guitar identity.

## Decision

**Option 1: Three Strings**

The reference cigar box guitar will use three strings.

Three strings provide sufficient musical range for the instrument's intended blues-focused role while preserving the simplicity, character and immediacy that make a cigar box guitar distinctive.

The configuration is particularly well suited to riffs, rhythm, slide and groove and should allow a relatively inexperienced player to develop simple but musically compelling material that showcases the instrument effectively.

The reduced range compared with four or six strings is an accepted trade-off.

For this reference instrument, additional strings would add capability that is not required by its mission and would dilute some of the clarity and minimalism that make the three-string format valuable.

## Consequences

This decision means that:

- the neck, nut, bridge and string spacing will be designed around three strings;
- total string tension will ultimately be determined by the chosen scale length, tuning, string gauges and string materials;
- the pickup system will need to sense three strings effectively;
- tuning becomes an important subsequent design decision because three strings can be arranged to support different musical and harmonic goals;
- the instrument's musical development will emphasise riffs, rhythm, slide, groove and economical use of notes;
- showcase material should be developed that demonstrates how musically compelling a deliberately simple three-string instrument can be;
- the reduced range is accepted as part of the instrument's character rather than treated as a deficiency;
- future reference or commissioned instruments may explore different string counts where their goals justify doing so.

## Future Revisit

The decision should be reviewed after the reference instrument has been completed and played for a meaningful period.

We should then ask:

- Did three strings provide enough musical range for the intended use?
- Did the instrument work particularly well for riffs, rhythm, slide and groove?
- Was it possible to develop simple but compelling showcase material?
- Did the three-string format improve the learning experience?
- Did the reduced string count contribute positively to the character of the instrument?
- Were there situations where a fourth string would have provided meaningful value?
- Would we choose three strings again for an instrument with similar goals?

## Sign-Off

**Approved.**

The reference cigar box guitar will use three strings, with future tuning, string selection and neck design decisions made within that constraint.

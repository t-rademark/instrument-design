# DD-008: Body Architecture

**Status:** Approved  
**Date:** 2026-08-20

## The Question

What body architecture should the reference cigar box guitar use?

The body must provide the recognisable visual identity of a cigar box guitar while also contributing meaningfully to the acoustic character of the instrument.

The reference instrument is primarily intended for amplified use, but DD-007 established that the resonant body should still make a noticeable contribution to the sound and physical playing experience.

The body therefore needs to balance:

- cigar box character;
- useful acoustic resonance;
- structural stability;
- practical construction;
- opportunities for learning during the reference build.

## Related Decisions

This decision builds particularly on:

- [`DD-006-neck-architecture.md`](DD-006-neck-architecture.md), which establishes the neck-through structural backbone;
- [`DD-007-bridge-coupling.md`](DD-007-bridge-coupling.md), which establishes that the bridge will excite a controlled, supported region of the soundboard.

The neck-through structure remains responsible for the primary structural geometry of the instrument.

The body is therefore free to be developed primarily as a resonant acoustic system around that backbone.

## Goals

The body should:

- remain unmistakably cigar-box-like in form;
- contribute meaningful acoustic character rather than acting merely as an enclosure;
- provide useful unplugged sound despite amplification being the primary mode of use;
- allow the soundboard to make the largest acoustic contribution;
- allow the back to contribute to the resonant behaviour of the body;
- use comparatively stiff sides to maintain body shape and couple the top and back;
- use solid timber for the principal resonant panels;
- allow the top and back to use different timbers where their different roles justify it;
- select timber primarily for suitability to its role rather than provenance or appearance alone;
- use Australian or Western Australian timber where it genuinely suits the requirement;
- retain straightforward box-like construction rather than evolving into a miniature conventional guitar body;
- provide a practical baseline from which future body designs can evolve.

## Design Space

### Option 1: Rigid Enclosure with Resonant Top

Build a relatively rigid box in which the soundboard provides most of the intentional acoustic response while the sides and back act primarily as an enclosure.

**Advantages**

- simple and predictable construction;
- strong dimensional stability;
- allows the soundboard to be treated as the principal acoustic variable;
- reduces demands on the back and sides.

**Disadvantages**

- limits the acoustic contribution of the body as a complete system;
- provides fewer opportunities to explore how the back and cavity influence the instrument;
- risks making much of the box acoustically passive.

### Option 2: Conventional Instrument-Style Resonant Body

Build the body more like a small conventional acoustic guitar, with individually designed top, back, sides, bracing and acoustic structure.

**Advantages**

- provides extensive control over acoustic behaviour;
- allows sophisticated optimisation of the resonant structure;
- offers substantial opportunities to learn conventional acoustic-instrument construction.

**Disadvantages**

- adds significant complexity;
- moves away from the simple character of cigar box guitar construction;
- risks turning the reference build into a miniature acoustic-guitar project;
- exceeds the acoustic requirements of an amplified-first instrument.

### Option 3: Resonant Wooden Box

Build a simple rectangular wooden body in which the top and back are intentionally resonant panels while the sides remain comparatively stiff and structural.

**Advantages**

- retains clear cigar box guitar identity;
- allows the entire body to contribute acoustically;
- keeps construction understandable;
- provides useful opportunities to explore soundboard and back behaviour;
- works naturally around the neck-through backbone;
- allows acoustic character without requiring conventional guitar-style body construction.

**Disadvantages**

- requires more consideration of materials and panel thickness than a rigid enclosure;
- introduces additional acoustic variables;
- the final response will be less predictable than a heavily engineered conventional acoustic body;
- suitable thin solid timber must be sourced.

## Acoustic Roles

The different parts of the box will not be expected to behave identically.

### Soundboard

The top will be the principal resonant panel.

It will:

- carry the bridge;
- receive vibration directly from the strings through the bridge;
- be designed for controlled movement;
- provide the largest intentional contribution to the body's acoustic response.

### Back

The back will also be treated as a resonant panel.

It need not behave identically to the soundboard, but it should be allowed to participate in the acoustic behaviour of the body rather than being made unnecessarily rigid.

### Sides

The sides will be comparatively stiff.

Their primary roles include:

- maintaining the shape of the body;
- joining and supporting the top and back;
- providing structural integrity;
- helping define the enclosed air cavity.

The aim is for the complete box to operate as an acoustic system without requiring every panel to flex to the same degree.

## Materials

The soundboard and back will be made from solid timber rather than plywood.

The two panels are not required to use the same timber species.

This allows each material to be selected according to the role of the panel rather than imposing visual or material symmetry on components with different jobs.

Timber selection will follow the principle:

> Choose timber for the job first; use Australian or Western Australian timber where it genuinely suits the job.

Acoustic performance takes priority over appearance for the soundboard.

Particularly attractive timber may therefore be better used elsewhere if another species provides more suitable soundboard properties.

The exact timber species remain open until suitable material has been identified and sourced.

## Body Form

The body will retain a simple rectangular cigar-box form rather than adopting the curved outline or construction methods of a conventional acoustic guitar.

The provisional external body dimensions are:

- **length: approximately 320 mm**
- **width: approximately 220 mm**
- **depth: approximately 75 mm**

These dimensions establish the intended body envelope rather than manufacturing tolerances.

They provide a compact, recognisably cigar-box-like body while offering enough soundboard area and internal air volume for meaningful acoustic contribution.

Detailed dimensions may be refined when the body structure, timber thicknesses and internal support arrangement are designed.

## Timber Sourcing

The exact soundboard and back timber species will not be selected until the required panel dimensions and suitable available material can be considered together.

The design will therefore avoid requiring a particular species merely because it is traditional.

Suitable luthier timber, smaller instrument blanks or appropriately selected offcuts may all be considered.

Availability is a legitimate design input, provided material performance remains the primary criterion.

## Trade-Offs

The principal trade-off is between **acoustic participation and construction simplicity**.

A rigid box would be easier to build and structurally predictable, but would provide less opportunity for the body to contribute to the instrument's character.

A conventionally engineered acoustic body could maximise acoustic potential, but would add considerable complexity and weaken the simple cigar-box identity of the instrument.

A resonant wooden box provides the useful middle ground.

The top and back are given genuine acoustic roles, the sides provide structural control, and the neck-through member remains responsible for the principal string-load structure.

The resulting body should be acoustically meaningful without pretending to be a conventional acoustic guitar.

## Decision

**Option 3: Resonant Wooden Box**

The reference cigar box guitar will use a simple rectangular wooden body designed as a resonant acoustic system.

The provisional external dimensions will be approximately:

**320 mm × 220 mm × 75 mm**

The body will use:

- a solid-wood resonant soundboard;
- a solid-wood resonant back;
- comparatively stiff sides.

The soundboard and back may use different timber species.

Timber will be selected according to functional suitability first, with Australian or Western Australian timber preferred where it performs appropriately.

The exact timber species and final panel thicknesses remain open pending material sourcing and further structural design.

## Consequences

This decision means that:

- the body dimensions are now sufficiently defined to begin evaluating suitable timber stock;
- the soundboard will be treated as the primary body-resonance component;
- the back will also be deliberately allowed to contribute acoustically;
- the sides will remain comparatively stiff and structural;
- solid timber must be sourced for the top and back;
- soundboard and back species may be selected independently;
- the neck-through backbone must be integrated without unnecessarily suppressing the resonant panels;
- internal support around the bridge must be developed within the 320 × 220 × 75 mm body envelope;
- exact panel thicknesses remain a later design question;
- soundboard species selection remains open until suitable material and dimensions are considered together;
- acoustic behaviour will become an explicit part of the completed reference instrument's evaluation.

## Future Revisit

The body architecture should be reviewed after the reference instrument has been completed and played both amplified and unplugged.

We should then ask:

- Does the body retain a strong cigar box guitar identity?
- Does the box make a noticeable acoustic contribution?
- Does the soundboard respond effectively to the bridge?
- Does the back contribute usefully?
- Are the sides sufficiently stiff?
- Is the unplugged sound enjoyable and useful?
- Does the 320 × 220 × 75 mm body feel comfortable to hold and play?
- Was solid timber worthwhile for the top and back?
- Did material availability cause useful or undesirable compromises?
- Would a larger, smaller, deeper or shallower body improve a future instrument?
- Would we choose the same body architecture again for an instrument with similar goals?

## Sign-Off

**Approved.**

The reference cigar box guitar will use an approximately 320 × 220 × 75 mm resonant wooden box with solid-wood top and back, comparatively stiff sides, and materials selected for their functional suitability while preserving the simple character of cigar box guitar construction.
